What you are looking at

Two metals in contact in seawater make a battery. The less noble one — here the aluminium — gives up electrons and dissolves; the more noble one, the stainless, does not corrode but provides somewhere for those electrons to go, by reducing dissolved oxygen at its surface.

That reaction is limited by how fast oxygen can reach the metal, so each square centimetre of stainless can only pass so much current. The total current the couple runs is therefore set by how much stainless is wetted — and nothing else.

All of that current has to come out of the aluminium. If the aluminium is large, the current is spread thinly over it and each part loses very little. If the aluminium is small, exactly the same current is concentrated into that small area, and it is eaten through quickly. Halve the anode and you double the rate; the damage follows the ratio of the two areas, not the identity of the metals.

The two things worth taking away

A small anode is the dangerous arrangement. An aluminium rivet in a stainless plate is a different object from a stainless rivet in an aluminium plate, even though a materials list would describe them identically. The first concentrates the whole couple current into the rivet.

This is why a coating on the anode can be worse than no coating at all. Paint the aluminium and leave a scratch, and you have not reduced the driving force — you have reduced the anode to the size of the scratch. The same current now leaves through a pinhole. Coat the cathode instead and the couple current falls with it.

What is not verified

Stated plainly, because a piece whose value is trustworthiness should not overclaim.

  • No engineer has reviewed this. The model is the standard oxygen-limited galvanic couple and the built-in checks confirm it reproduces the closed-form area-ratio law exactly — but that check is the author's own, not a corrosion engineer's.
  • The cathode is assumed fully oxygen-limited, at one current density for still, aerated seawater. Real limiting currents vary several-fold with temperature, flow and fouling, and a couple can be under mixed control instead, in which case the ratio law is weaker than the strict proportionality drawn here.
  • The attack is drawn as uniform thinning. Real galvanic attack on aluminium is usually pitting, which perforates sooner than an averaged depth suggests — so the real case is worse than the one drawn, not better.
  • Resistance of the electrolyte path is ignored. In seawater that is a fair simplification; in fresh water or damp concrete it is not, and distance from the couple starts to matter a great deal.
  • No passivation, no protective film, no polarity reversal. Aluminium in particular depends on a passive film that this model does not represent, and some couples reverse polarity with temperature.
  • Time is compressed heavily, and it has to be: the mild settings take decades. What you watch in a second is about a week.
