What you are looking at

A short, stocky strut fails by squashing. It carries load until the steel yields, so its capacity is the yield strength times the area — and a stronger grade buys you exactly the extra strength you paid for.

A long, slender strut fails a different way. Long before the steel is anywhere near yielding, the column stops being able to stay straight: any small bow grows rather than resisting, and it folds. What decides that load is the stiffness of the steel and the geometry of the section. Not its strength.

Every structural steel has essentially the same stiffness. A high-grade steel is not stiffer than a cheap one — it just yields later. So in the slender range the three grades give the same answer, and the extra money bought a number on a certificate.

The two things worth taking away

Past the transition, specifying a higher grade does nothing. The dots merge. If a column is failing its check and it is slender, a stronger steel is not the fix — a fatter section, a stiffer one, or a shorter unrestrained length is.

Length is punished twice over. Capacity falls with the square of the slenderness: double the unrestrained length and you have a quarter of the column. That is why a bit of restraint at mid-height is worth more than almost anything you can do to the material.

What is not verified

Stated plainly, because a piece whose value is trustworthiness should not overclaim.

  • No engineer has reviewed this. The model is the Euler critical stress against a squash limit, and the built-in checks confirm it reproduces the closed forms exactly — but that check is the author's own, not a structural engineer's.
  • This is the perfect-column idealisation. Real columns have initial bow, residual stresses from rolling or welding, and load that is never perfectly concentric. Design codes therefore use buckling curves that sit well below the Euler line near the transition, so the real capacity around the knee is lower than drawn.
  • The transition is drawn as a corner. In reality the two modes blend over a broad band of slenderness, and the worst place to be is inside that band, which is exactly where this drawing is least accurate.
  • One end condition, one axis, one section. Effective length depends on how the ends are held and real sections buckle about their weak axis, or in torsion, before the strong axis is troubled.
  • Stiffness is taken as identical across grades. It is very nearly so for structural steels, which is the point being made — but it is not exactly so, and it is not true at all if you change material family.
