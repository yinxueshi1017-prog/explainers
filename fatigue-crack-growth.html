What you are looking at

A crack in a steel joint grows a little on every load cycle. How much depends on how hard the crack tip is being worked, which depends on the stress range and on how deep the crack already is. Both of those raise the rate, so growth accelerates: the crack spends a long time being small and a short time being large. That is the shape of the curve on the right, and it is why a crack that has been harmless for years is not reassuring.

Two sizes matter. There is the depth at which an inspector could actually find it — below that, it is there and nobody can see it. And there is the depth at which the remaining section can no longer carry the load. The span between them is the entire opportunity to do anything about it.

That span is measured in cycles, and it falls as the cube of the stress range. Doubling the stress range does not halve your chances; it divides them by eight.

The two things worth taking away

Total life is the wrong number to manage. A joint can have a long life and still be uninspectable, because almost all of that life is spent below the size anyone can find. What an inspection programme is buying is the window — and the window can be short while the life is long.

The fraction of life spent undetectable barely moves. Change the stress range and the total life changes enormously, but the proportion of it spent hidden stays about the same. So you cannot inspect your way out of a stress problem: lowering the stress range buys real time, while inspecting more often only helps if a window exists to inspect inside.

What is not verified

Stated plainly, because a piece whose value is trustworthiness should not overclaim.

  • No engineer has reviewed this. The model is the Paris law with the Barsom mean-line constants for ferrite-pearlite steels, and the built-in checks confirm the integration reproduces the closed form exactly — but that check is the author's own, not a fracture-mechanics engineer's.
  • A mean line is not a design line. Real crack growth data scatters by a factor of two or more about it, and assessment codes deliberately use an upper bound rather than the mean. Treated as design guidance, the numbers here are optimistic.
  • One crack, one shape, one constant geometry factor. Real cracks change aspect ratio as they grow, interact with each other, and sit in stress fields that the single factor used here does not describe.
  • No threshold and no closure. Below a certain stress intensity range cracks effectively stop growing, and this model keeps growing them, which is conservative. It also ignores retardation after overloads, which is not.
  • Detection is drawn as a clean line. Real inspection has a probability of detection that rises gradually with size — an inspection inside the window can still miss, which makes the real case worse than the one drawn.
  • The critical size is taken as the section depth over most of this range, with the fracture-limited size used when it is smaller. Real failure may come sooner, by plastic collapse of the remaining ligament.
